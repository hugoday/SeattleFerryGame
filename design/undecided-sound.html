Seattle Ferry Inc · game design · the 10-hour campaign

The Undecided Sound

The Salish Sea went undecided years ago. Your two hulls work the last lit pocket of it. Push the light back out, basin by basin, and find out what put it out. A reclamation campaign built on prototype2's observation economy, with a hub-and-spoke freight network as its skeleton: freighters on the deep trunk lines, skiffs on the shallow spokes, and attention — the one thing that doesn't scale — spent where the light ends.

Hour three, as the log would tell it: a basin opens. Convoy, crossing, restoration, first freighter, gold rush — the campaign's heartbeat, once per basin, harder every time.

§0Where this stands

what prototype 2 proved, and where it stalls

Prototype 2's machinery is good — the tick that only moves when spent, the commit screen's exact quotes, the radar/sonar honesty flip, buoys as a subscription. What it lacks is plumbing and a destination. A headless bot playing the dumbest viable freight strategy, never once observing, pinging, or buying a buoy:

$10k → $80kmean over 5 seeds, 400 ticks, ignoring the observation game entirely
12–23×per run a hull docks into an apron not worth fuel, then waits out the 16-tick rotation
7×spread in final credits between seeds, on offer RNG alone

The systems don't demand each other, and the session has no shape. This document is the game where they do, and it does: a campaign of roughly ten hours with a beginning, an escalation, and an ending worth reaching.

§1The game: reopen the Sound

the campaign in one paragraph

The current 4-port map becomes the home pocket — the first hour of five basins. Beyond every dark headland the chart is stale, ports sit unlit with hungry populations, and the water is exactly what prototype2 already simulates: unstable, component-chewing, haunted. Each basin follows a rhythm the systems already support — scout it (the in-hull captain game: creep in quiet, ping, read lying instruments), open it (the expedition of §5), work it (desperation rates decaying to normal — every basin has a gold rush), stabilize it (stations, circuits), hand it to the agents, and move your attention to the next frontier. It is the Factorio arc — manual, then automated, then expand — mapped onto this game's own theme: attention is singular, so the only way to reach the frontier is to make the rear run without you.

The mystery is the throttle. Every reopened port holds a fragment — harbor logs, a survivor, the wrecks of the old ferry fleet — and the storyideas.txt S.C.A.N. fiction is the voice for all of it. The player's real fuel isn't credits. It's what's in basin four?

§2The certainty loop

the economy underneath everything

Decided water is the commodity. Freight consumes it, traffic produces it, the anomaly destroys it, and the player is the only one who can spend attention on purpose.

Five rules wire prototype2's existing systems into that loop. They are the foundations everything after §3 stands on:

the five foundations

  • Commerce is coverage. Every hull underway decides the water she moves through (moving coverage, 3 NM; a 10-tick stability floor of 2 in her track). Busy lanes stay settled; abandoned lanes go feral. Incidents pause traffic → water undecides → the spiral.
  • The anomaly hunts the seams. Lit stations and ports repel it while lit; it prowls the frontier of your paid-for light, leaves a 4 NM wake capped at stability 1, and fouls stations dark in passing. Re-lighting is a tender errand: a hull, 1 tick on station, $800.
  • Attention has customers. The Array Office posts SURVEY (hold a patch at ≥3 for N ticks), FIX (a ping-verified read on the contact), and ESCORT (keep a client inside your coverage, 1.5× rate) on the same aprons, in the same two currencies: dollars and ticks.
  • Noise is a tool. The contact is noise-drawn (already in the code, NOISE_DRAWS=25) — so loud is a choice. A flanking, pinging skiff drags its wander south while the freight slips the corridor. Fragile cargo pays you to manage where its attention goes.
  • The story is a contract. Three verified reads from bearings ≥45° apart triangulate the contact. Each is a FIX you were paid for anyway — the story rides the economy instead of interrupting it.

§3The network: trunks, spokes, and the yard between

hub-and-spoke — the pocket planes structure, made maritime

Each basin has exactly one deep-water port. Big ships run the trunk lines between deep-water ports; small ships work the shallow spokes from the hub to final destinations. The hierarchy isn't advisory — it's enforced by a single stat:

draft, and the fleet hierarchy

  • Draft: one number per class, checked against a shallow-water layer on the existing land map. Deep-draft hulls can only berth at deep-water ports. No rule-lawyering — the freighter physically cannot make the last mile.
  • Spoke classes (shallow draft): Skiff, Cutter, Lighter — the existing boats, 2–6 hold, cheap, nimble, at home in bad water if handled well.
  • Trunk classes (deep draft): Coaster (10 hold, 11 kts) and Freighter (18–24 hold, 12 kts, array-grade radar 12 NM). Her safety is her array, not armor.

Staging finds its true purpose

Staging — unmotivated since prototype 1 — is the system this structure was waiting for. Keep it only at deep-water ports, where it becomes the container yard between trunk and spoke: a freighter drops twelve items into stage; skiffs pick them apart by destination over the next day. At spoke ports it disappears entirely. One glance at a hub's STAGED section answers the network question — is my Sound flowing, or silting up?

Time without the cliff: soft windows

Contract expiration as prototype2 plays it — penalty, standing loss, VOID bookkeeping — is three punishments for one lapse. Cut the cliff, keep the clock: standard freight never explodes. It's a depressed economy; shippers are glad anyone ships. Pay decays gently past the quoted window (to a floor of ~60%) — late is disappointing, never catastrophic. Hard windows survive only where they're opted into and paid for: priority and Office contracts, clearly marked. And perishables (below) carry time pressure diegetically — salmon spoils; nobody needs a rules lawyer.

The routing dilemma stays intact: a contract from one basin's far shore to another's still ages through four legs — spoke → stage → trunk → spoke — so transshipment is the patient route and a lone skiff running the direct crossing is the fast, dicey one. One fairness requirement: quoted windows come from network time (shortest path over the trunk+spoke graph plus ~4 ticks handling per transfer), never crow-flight — or hub routing feels rigged against the player.

Freight that carries the world: tags, not SKUs

The contents list stops being flavor text. Post-collapse Salish Sea freight — diesel drums, transformer coils, dock pilings, relief flour, medical supplies, nets, smoked salmon, cider, generator parts, mail — sorted into five category tags that do mechanical work without an item simulator:

cargo tags — and how they map onto the fleet

  • MATERIEL — feeds restoration manifests. Heavy and patient: the natural cargo of large, slow trunk hulls.
  • HEAVY — freighter-only, full stop. Reinforces the hierarchy without a rule in sight.
  • PERISHABLE — the home of honest time pressure, and the natural cargo of small, fast spoke hulls. The tag system quietly assigns each ship class its role.
  • FRAGILE — anomaly-sensitive; deresolves if the contact closes within 6 NM. Pays you to manage where its attention goes.
  • MAIL — tiny, cheap, high standing — and the lore channel: letters as found fiction.

Other people on the network

NPC coasters ply the lit trunk lines — texture, not dependents. They never need rescuing; the player is the only one who goes out there, and that identity is worth protecting. But they earn their place three ways:

what npc traffic does

  • They keep the water decided. Their moving coverage feeds the certainty loop — more stability → more traffic → more stability. A reopened basin that draws service back starts maintaining itself: returning traffic is the basin's visible health bar, and the rear heals instead of nagging.
  • They pay tolls. A commission (~$40) per NPC transit of your lit line segments, licensed and settled through the Array Office — lighthouse dues, essentially. Buoys become two-sided infrastructure: upkeep out, tolls in. Settled once a market day as a single line (“Harrow line: 11 transits, +$440”), so the toll figure doubles as the basin's health readout.
  • The reward is freedom from drag, not a revenue stream. Tune a mature basin's tolls to 80–110% of its upkeep — never meaningfully above. Healing the rear should stop it costing you money and attention; it must never out-earn sailing, or the optimal play becomes lighting stations to farm traffic, which uses none of the game's verbs. (At $6/tick upkeep and $40/transit, three or four coasters on circuits land near 70–90% unforced.) The ramp as service returns also smooths the post-rush hour: the gold rush fades while tolls rise.
  • Their withdrawal is the storm warning. “KESTREL suspends service” telegraphs danger diegetically — no tutorial box, no rescue obligation.

Two rules keep them background, not opponents: their routing stays dumb (service frequency scales with stability and standing — you shape traffic by making water trustworthy, never by gaming a pathfinder), and they never take offers off your aprons. Their trade is abstract tonnage; the toll is your cut of it.

Hub agents, from day one

The tedium risk in hub-and-spoke is per-item transfer clicking across five basins. So the T2 agent's “load whatever serves the circuit” logic extends to hubs: staged cargo bound for a basin auto-flows onto that basin's circuit hulls. The player designs the network and handles exceptions; agents run the steady state. This ships with the hub, not after it — and a silting alarm (stage >80% for 20 ticks) is an interrupt worth waking for.

Basins have economic identities

A timber basin, a fishery basin, an industrial yard — each weighting its export mix through the tags above: materiel flows north on slow trunk hulls, perishables sprint the spokes. Trunk flows become directional — full south, light north — and balancing backhauls is the endgame optimization for players who want it, while every basin gets a personality that isn't just its hazard.

connects ship classes, staging, windows, agents, and the basin map into one structure — and gives the fleet a reason to be a fleet.

§4The moving lighthouse and the convoy

the highest magic-per-line-of-code in the design

Convoys already exist in the stability math. Hulls contribute radar coverage; coverage stacks; ships sailing together are mechanically a moving pocket of decided water. The game needs exactly one new verb — commit as convoy: same leg, paced to the slowest hull, coverage pooled, one interrupt stream, any hull detachable back to her own orders. Small ships huddling across big water is emergent from code that is already written.

And the freighter is a moving lighthouse. Her 12 NM array decides the water as she goes, and the corridor she walks holds a stability floor of 3 for 6 ticks after her passage — so skiffs can draft the trunk behind her. Scheduling spoke movements around trunk departures gives every hub a “train is leaving” rhythm the whole basin organizes around. It also answers why the trunk is safe without making it boring: safety is a scheduled, purchasable, interdictable event, not a property of the map.

connects big and small hulls into one tactical system, and — late game — gives the anomaly its endgame move: interdict a trunk line, dim the lighthouse, and make the company sail together again.

§5Opening a basin

the expedition — the campaign's heartbeat, once per basin

A new basin's deep-water port is broken. No trunk service until it's restored — and restoring it is a logistics project, not a scripted mission:

the expedition loop

  • Scout. In-hull, quiet, one skiff: find the deep berth, survey the approach, learn the local hazard. The chart is 12 years stale; what it shows and what's there have drifted apart.
  • Assemble. Port repair needs a manifest — cedar logs, transformers, boat parts (the CONTENTS list finally means something). Accumulate it at the nearest hub. This is freight work: the campaign's biggest beats are made of its smallest verbs.
  • Cross. Commit the convoy — small ships in trunk water, pooled coverage against a crossing rated for freighters. Pick your moment against the contact's position. Lose a hull and a third of the repair materials sits in a derelict in bad water: the expedition grows a rescue subplot, not a game over.
  • Restore. Materials ashore, ticks in the berth, the pier lights section by section. Then the payoff beat: the first freighter comes over the horizon.
  • Rush. Reopened ports post desperation rates (×2+, decaying over ~an hour as trade resumes). Every basin has a gold rush; the rush funds the next push. Then circuits take over, and your attention moves on.

Every deep-water port is a shipyard

The Shipyard stops being a special place — yard facilities are what a deep-water port is: repairs, refits, the buoy tender, trunk berths, a graving dock. Every basin becomes structurally identical (one deep port plus spokes), the special-case SHY code disappears, and restoring a basin's deep port restores a full-service yard on the frontier — which matters, because the frontier is where hulls get hurt.

The basin's freighter is a wreck in the basin

How does the player ever afford a trunk hull? They don't — they earn one, in three beats that use systems already built: restore the deep port (the expedition above), tow her in — the basin's old freighter is derelict somewhere in local water, and the tow verb exists — then rebuild her in the graving dock you just fixed. Port → tow → rebuild staggers the payoff across the whole gold-rush hour, and the grant varies by basin (a freighter here, a coaster there, a special hull, a dry-dock capability) so hour eight isn't five identical ships deep.

Then repair the spokes, port by port

The deep port opens the trunk; each spoke port is its own smaller restoration project — the original readme's oldest idea, finally home. Staged so the basin comes online in visible steps: crane first (the port can unload), then apron (it posts offers), then the local station (its water lights). Restoration manifests are the demand sink that makes MATERIEL flow from day one. The repetition risk across ~a dozen spoke ports is handled by varying conditions, not mechanics: one port is intact but abandoned, one is wrecked to the waterline, one is inhabited but dark — and wary of you.

Overextension is the campaign's failure pressure: push while the rear's upkeep lapses and stations go dark behind you, circuits pause, and you get called home. Frontier versus rear is the strategic game — and it is made of attention, which is the theme, priced.

§6The campaign

ten hours, five basins, one answer

Each basin has a fixed identity — the hazard tiers are prototype2's own “Not yet” list, promoted from roadmap bullets to basin personalities — but the order is the player's. After the home pocket, the three middle basins open in any sequence; only the Source gates behind the rest. Crossing difficulty scales naturally with distance from your lit network, so the far choice is the bold choice, not a locked door. Chapter cards name each opening (“II. HARROW REACH”) — ten hours needs punctuation, and which chapter is “II” is up to you:

The fleet is the emotional anchor

You don't buy ships from a menu — you mostly salvage them, one per basin, each with a name, scars, and the story of where she was lost (§5's port → tow → rebuild chain). Six to eight hulls by hour eight, most on circuits, each one earned. Derelict-and-tow gives losses weight without permadeath: she's recoverable, but the recovery is an expedition into the water that took her. And every hull keeps a logbook — legs run, tonnage delivered, damage survived, where she was raised — a dozen lines of bookkeeping that turns hulls into characters. Attachment is the cheapest retention mechanic there is.

The engine of want, and the ending

No debt, no loan, no creditor ticking in the corner — the campaign itself is the money sink. Expedition manifests and restorations are the capex that savings exist for, and desperation rates decay, so sitting still has opportunity cost without a single tax: the pull of the next basin replaces the push of a lender. Operating drain stays where it already lives — fuel and station upkeep — and station tolls (§3) let a healthy lane pay for itself. One design watch: if the mid-game goes slack once a rush fades, the fix is a more expensive next manifest, not a meter. The ending: reach and resolve the Source — chart it, starve it, or feed it — with an epilogue that reads back your whole Sound: tonnage moving, ports lit, hulls lost, lines back in service. Ten hours, done, satisfied. Replay comes free from basin order and the ending choice; don't design for more than that.

§7The rhythm

dead air, and the emotional ledger

Kill the 16-tick lump. Each port rolls for one new offer every 4 ticks (~35%); a “market day” every 32 keeps the ritual. Thin aprons surface spot charters (1.3–1.5×, tight windows) — docking into nothing should feel like an opportunity, not a bus stop. And with the Office posting surveys and tenders, the idle moment always has a second market.

Balance the ledger. The game currently has one positive beat (docking pay) and nine kinds of punishment. Add: standing tier-ups announced with their perk (60: priority contracts; 75: shippers cover fuel on priority runs), bundle jackpots celebrated in the log's own voice, first-time feats (“first freighter south in nine years”), delivery streaks per hull. Cheap lines of fiction, disproportionate warmth — and the fiction is this project's best tool.

Respect the ten hours. Circuits must be trustworthy enough to forget; the interrupt list stays small and worth waking for; and a captain's log screen tracks story fragments and open contracts so a player returning after three days doesn't lose the thread.

The network view is one more press of minus. The map already zooms (-/+ RANGE, SCALE STRATEGIC in the header) — keep zooming out past strategic and the chart becomes the NETWORK scale: basins as nodes, trunk lines as edges, circuits and their hulls in motion, stage occupancy per hub, lanes lit or dark, tolls ticking. One glance answers “is my Sound flowing or silting?” — and it's where silting alarms land. It unlocks with the second basin, so hour one stays a single screen.

§9Design gates

the playtest bot becomes the referee

The repo already has the right culture — checks.py, fuzz.py — so the design goals are assertions, refereed by headless bots after every balance pass:

accept the design when

  • The freight-only bot (ignores observation, as today) sees its margins go negative by tick ~250 in at least 4 of 5 seeds, as untended lanes destabilize under it. Ignoring the water must stop being viable.
  • A bot that also re-lights stations and takes surveys stays solvent and can fund its first expedition manifest by tick ~300.
  • Convoy gate: three skiffs crossing trunk water together take under 1/3 the component damage of three solo crossings — the pooled-coverage math, verified.
  • Window gate: ≥90% of hub-routed priority offers are deliverable in-window at the posted quote when the network is nominal — transshipment must never be rigged.
  • Toll gate: with NPC service restored, a mature basin's station tolls cover 80–110% of its upkeep — self-sustaining, never a business. A bot that lights stations and stops sailing must trend broke.
  • Flow gate: with T2 circuits running, hub stage occupancy stays below cap indefinitely — no silting under steady state.
  • Median RETURN run in the mid-game is 4–10 ticks — something worth waking for, that often, no oftener.

§10What doesn't change

The tick that only moves when spent. The commit screen's exact, honest quotes. The honesty flip with no confirmation. Buoys as subscription. The log's voice. And the strategic/tactical zoom, which resolves the oldest question in storyideas.txt for good: you get the tycoon and the captain's chair — the map is the dispatcher, the in-hull view is the chair, and the campaign is what finally makes both of them matter. The finished company is two or three freighters on trunk schedules, six or eight small hulls on basin circuits, and you at the frontier, spending the one thing that doesn't scale.
